Mission and scope

The goal is a 3-motor tilt-rotor VTOL that hovers stably and tilts its front motors on command. A full transition to forward flight is the stretch goal, not the pass mark.

  • Must have: stable hover in QHOVER, tilt mechanism working on the bench and in flight, live telemetry on the dashboard for every flight.
  • Stretch: first transition from hover to forward flight and back.
  • Reference layout: the E-flite Convergence, which ArduPilot supports natively:
    • Two front motors on servo tilt mounts: pointing up for hover, forward for cruise. Small differential tilt gives yaw in hover.
    • One fixed rear motor for hover only, on the tail boom. It switches off in forward flight.
    • Conventional tail on a carbon boom: the NACA 4412 wing can't fly as a flying wing.
    • Fully 3D-printed wing on a carbon spar, with printed pods, tilt mounts and joints.
    • Pixhawk Cube flight controller (Cube Orange+) running ArduPilot Plane (QuadPlane, tricopter frame, vectored tilt).
    • Live MAVLink telemetry to a browser dashboard, so the aircraft can be watched and commanded from the ground: arm, change modes, send it to a point, bring it home.
  • Rule: every flight needs a clear go/no-go call. No props on during bench work, no untethered first hover. A pilot with an RC transmitter is always in command, even when the ground station is sending commands.

Design process

Size the aircraft from the parts we already have, in this order. Each step feeds the next, and the CG is the number everything hangs on.

  1. Mass budget. Weigh every part: motors, ESCs, 2 MG995s, battery, Cube with its carrier board, GPS, receiver, telemetry radio and antenna, video transmitter and camera if fitted, wiring. Add 40–50% for the airframe and prints. That sum is the target all-up weight (AUW).
  2. Thrust check. Clamp one motor with its prop on a kitchen scale, throttle it with a servo tester, and record thrust and current at 50%, 75% and 100%. Hover needs a thrust-to-weight ratio of at least 2, so hover sits near 50–60% throttle.
  3. Wing sizing. Target a wing loading of 35–45 g/dm² (VTOLs run heavy). Example: 1400 g at 40 g/dm² gives 35 dm², which at a 1.2 m span is a 29 cm mean chord.
  4. Motor placement. Front motors sit a distance a ahead of the CG, the rear motor b behind it. With equal thrust per motor, the rear arm is twice the front offset (b = 2a). Front prop tips need at least 2 cm clearance from each other and the fuselage.
  5. Match the cruise CG to the hover CG. Flying wing: CG at 15–25% of the mean aerodynamic chord (MAC); conventional wing: 25–30% MAC. Put the hover balance point from step 4 exactly on this spot. Most homemade tilt-rotors fail here.
  6. Tilt mechanism. Covered in its own section below.
  7. Power budget. Hover current is 3 times the per-motor current at hover thrust. Check the battery C rating covers it, ESCs are rated at least 1.3× the measured peak, and the servos run off their own 5–6 V BEC rated 5 A or more.

Why it's laid out like this

  • A tail, not a flying wing. The NACA 4412 is cambered and pitches nose-down on its own, so it can't fly tailless. A boom and tail fix that, and the boom gives the rear motor a home.
  • Hover balance. With equal thrust from each motor, the two front motors at distance a ahead of the CG balance the rear motor at 2a behind it. I picked a = 180 as the smallest value that keeps the rear prop clear of the wing.
  • Tail size. Tail volume coefficients of about 0.45 (horizontal) and 0.035 (vertical) are normal for a stable model. With a 650 mm tail arm that gives 0.052 m² and 0.019 m².
  • Battery in the nose. The rear motor, boom and tail all sit far behind the CG. About 400 g of battery, Cube and power electronics has to sit ahead of the wing to balance them. Use the calculator to check as parts are weighed.
  • Control surfaces. Ailerons on the outer wing, plus an elevator and a rudder on the tail. That's four 9 g servos that weren't on the original parts list.

From layout to CAD

  1. Master sketch. In Onshape (free, runs in the browser) or Fusion 360, make one top-view sketch with named variables: span, chord, cg_x, a, y_front, prop_d, tail_arm. Draw the lines in the figure above from them. Every part references this sketch, so moving the CG moves everything.
  2. Airfoil. Ready-made NACA 4412 at 250 mm chord, leading edge at 0,0: DXF (Onshape, Fusion: Insert DXF), CSV (Fusion's ImportSplineCSV script) or .dat (unit chord). Thickness is 30 mm at the spar station. Cut the spar hole at 28% chord and a second alignment-pin hole near the trailing edge.
  3. Parts, in print order: tilt pod and mount (bench-test it with an MG995 first), one wing section to weigh, the centre section with the spar sleeve and front pod mounts, the remaining wing sections, the nose pod (sized around the battery, the Cube and the GPS mast), the boom clamp, the rear motor mount, then the tail surfaces.
  4. Weigh as you go. Put every printed part on the scale, enter it in the calculator, and tick "weighed". Re-check the CG and the battery position before gluing anything permanent.

3D-printed wing

A printed wing will be heavier than foam, so weigh a test section before trusting the mass budget. Print time on one printer is also the longest task in the build.

  • Material: LW-PLA (foaming PLA) for the skins and ribs. Use normal PLA+ or PETG only where loads go in: root ribs, spar sleeves, motor pod mounts.
  • Structure: thin printed shells with internal ribs, carried by a carbon tube spar running through every section. Add a second, smaller rod or pin per joint so sections can't rotate on the spar.
  • Load path: the front motor pods and the rear motor boom bolt to the spar, never to the skin. In hover, the whole aircraft hangs from these points.
  • Sections: size each one to fit the print bed (often about 200 mm). Number them, print in order, and dry-fit on the spar before gluing.
  • Airfoil: NACA 4412 (12% thick, cambered), which needs the tail shown in the layout. Mount the wing at 2–3° incidence to the boom.
  • Weight check: print one section first, weigh it, and multiply by the section count. If the wing alone pushes hover throttle above about 60%, thin the walls or shorten the span before printing the rest.
  • Joining: epoxy or thick CA, with sections aligned on the spar so there is no twist. Check both tips sit at the same angle before the glue sets.
  • Printer time: tilt mounts and pods are small and needed first for bench tests. Queue the wing sections right after, and keep the printer running around the clock.

Tilt mechanism with MG995

MG995s are a good fit. They give roughly 9–11 kg·cm of torque against a worst-case need of about 1–2 kg·cm. Their weak spots are gear slop and centring, not strength.

Why the torque is enough

Rough numbers for a 10-inch prop:

  • Motor, prop and mount weigh about 100 g. With the pivot within 1 cm of their centre of mass, the static load is about 0.1 kg·cm.
  • The biggest load is gyroscopic: tilting a spinning prop. At about 7000 rpm and a 90°/s tilt rate it comes to roughly 1 kg·cm. Fast yaw corrections can spike higher, still well under the rating.

Any angle in between

The MG995 is a positional servo, and ArduPilot commands it continuously:

  • In hover the motors sit near vertical, and ArduPilot moves each one a few degrees independently for yaw (vectored yaw).
  • During transition it sweeps both forward smoothly at the rate you set (Q_TILT_RATE_UP / Q_TILT_RATE_DN), holding intermediate angles as airspeed builds.
  • Q_TILT_MAX caps how far forward they tilt while still in a VTOL mode. Full 90° only happens once the transition completes.
  • You calibrate the mapping: set SERVOx_MIN / MAX / TRIM so ArduPilot's "vertical" and "fully forward" land exactly at 0° and 90° on the real mount.

Design rules for the mount

  • The servo only twists; it never carries the motor's weight or thrust. Run the pivot as a shaft (an M3 or M4 bolt, or a small bearing) supported on both sides, with the servo driving it.
  • Put the pivot axis through the combined centre of mass of the motor and prop.
  • Allow travel from about 5–10° back past vertical to 90° forward. Check the MG995 reaches that range in the PWM window you use; many give only about 120° at 1000–2000 µs.
  • Hard stops go just beyond the commanded range, never inside it. A servo stalled against a stop overheats and strips.
  • Clamp the horn with a screw (a metal horn if you have one). The plastic spline strips under vibration.
  • Analog servos: keep their output at 50 Hz.

Watch for

  • Gear slop and deadband. Wiggle the motor by hand; anything over about 1–2° of play shows up as hover wobble. Pick the two tightest servos from your stock and keep a third as a spare.
  • Clone quality. Bench-cycle each servo for 10 minutes before fitting it.
  • Current. Each MG995 can pull over 1.5 A under load. A weak BEC browns out the flight controller mid-air, so power servos from a dedicated BEC rated 5 A or more.

Cube flight controller

The brain is our Cube Orange+ running ArduPilot Plane. The Cube's IMUs sit on a heated, vibration-isolated board inside the cube, and it plugs into a carrier board that holds all the connectors.

  • Carrier board: ours is the Standard Set's ADS-B carrier board, which has every port used below. It also has a built-in ADS-B receiver that picks up nearby manned aircraft (optional, see the parameter table).
  • Size and weight: the Cube on its carrier is much bigger and heavier than a small racing-style controller. Weigh yours, put it on the mass sheet, and design the fuselage bay around it, with room for the cables to bend.
  • Mounting: the IMUs are already isolated, so mount the carrier firmly on foam pads, arrow facing forward. Don't add a second wobbly soft mount. After the first hover, check vibration in the log (VIBE): below about 30 m/s² and no clipping.
  • Firmware: in Mission Planner, Install Firmware → Plane → board CubeOrangePlus (not CubeOrange: the two need different firmware), latest stable release.
  • GPS: a CubePilot Here 3+ or Here 4 on CAN1, on a mast away from the power wires and the video transmitter. Its button is the safety switch: press it before arming.

Electronics and ArduPilot setup

Flash ArduPilot Plane (not Copter) and build the whole system on a bench board with props off before anything goes into the airframe. Check every parameter below against the ArduPilot Tiltrotor and E-flite Convergence setup pages for your firmware version. Names and motor numbering change between releases.

Wiring

  • Power module on the Cube's POWER1, plus a separate 5–6 V BEC (5 A or more) feeding the servo rail
  • 3 ESCs on MAIN 1–3, 2 MG995s on MAIN 5–6, ailerons on MAIN 7–8, elevator and rudder on AUX 1–2 (see the output map)
  • RC receiver on TELEM2 (CRSF) or RCIN (SBUS), with failsafe set
  • Here 3+ / Here 4 GPS on CAN1, on a mast away from the power wires and the VTX
  • Telemetry radio on TELEM1, antenna vertical and clear of the carbon spar
  • No airspeed sensor for now: ARSPD_TYPE = 0, and leave the I2C port free for one later
  • Cube carrier mounted firmly, arrow forward (no extra soft mount)

Bench sequence

  1. Calibrate the accelerometer, compass and radio.
  2. Run Mission Planner's motor test with props off. Confirm each motor's number, position and spin direction; the front pair counter-rotate.
  3. In QSTABILIZE, tilt the aircraft by hand and check that the motors and tilt servos respond in the correcting direction.
  4. Command yaw and confirm the front motors tilt in opposite directions.
  5. Switch to FBWA and confirm both motors swing to fully forward and the rear motor stops. Tilt the aircraft by hand and check the ailerons, elevator and rudder move to correct it.
  6. Connect the dashboard over the telemetry radio. Check that the mode, attitude, battery and tilt angles match the aircraft, and that a mode change sent from the dashboard shows up.
  7. Fly the same configuration in SITL until the pilot can hover and transition there. The dashboard's Demo mode is useful practice for the ground station operator.

Flying without an airspeed sensor

The Cube has no airspeed sensor built in. The IMU measures acceleration and rotation, and the GPS measures speed over the ground, but the wing only cares about speed through the air. ArduPilot estimates airspeed by subtracting its wind estimate from the GPS ground speed. That works, but the estimate is weakest during transitions and low, slow flight, so these rules replace the sensor:

  • Always transition into the wind. Into the wind, ground speed is lower than airspeed, so the estimate errs on the safe side. Downwind it errs the dangerous way: the aircraft thinks it's fast enough when the wing is close to stalling.
  • The transition is already speed- and time-gated. ArduPilot holds the front motors partly tilted until the estimate passes AIRSPEED_MIN, and Q_TRANS_FAIL sends it back to VTOL if that takes too long. You don't need custom code for this.
  • Fly with margin. Cruise at 15–16 m/s, about twice the stall speed, instead of 13. Climb before turning downwind, and avoid slow, low turns.
  • Calm days first. The first transitions happen in under about 3 m/s of wind, at 30 m or more, in FBWA with the pilot holding high throttle.
  • Practise in SITL with wind. Set ARSPD_TYPE = 0 and SIM_WIND_SPD = 4–5, and fly transitions both into the wind and downwind to see the difference.
  • Use the dashboard's wind readout. It shows ArduPilot's wind estimate and whether the aircraft has a headwind or a tailwind right now. Start a transition only with a headwind.
  • Upgrade path: a generic MS4525DO clone on I2C, or an analog MPXV7002DP pitot kit on the carrier's ADC pin (check the pin's voltage range first), costs a fraction of a branded sensor and is far better than none.

Radio links and telemetry

A "completely controllable" aircraft uses up to three separate radio links. Each does one job, so losing one never takes out the others.

Link Band Carries Hardware
RC control 2.4 GHz The pilot's sticks and mode switch. This is the safety link. ExpressLRS (ELRS) transmitter and receiver, or whatever transmitter the pilot already flies
Telemetry (MAVLink) 915 MHz or 433 MHz, depending on country Everything on the dashboard, plus commands from the ground: arm, modes, fly-to-point, RTL A pair of SiK radios (Holybro SiK V3 or similar) to start. An RFD900x pair for long range.
Video (optional) 5.8 GHz A live camera view with on-screen data Analog VTX and goggles, or a digital system (DJI O3, Walksnail)

Which frequency? 5.8 GHz is for video, not telemetry. There's no standard 935 MHz telemetry band, so you probably mean 915 MHz, which is the licence-free band in the Americas and Australia. Europe, the UK, India and many other countries use 433 MHz (or 868 MHz) instead. Look up your country's licence-free bands and power limits first, then buy the matching radio version.

Recommended setup

  • Start with: ELRS for RC, a SiK telemetry pair in your country's band, and no video. That covers live telemetry and full control from the dashboard.
  • Add later: 5.8 GHz video, with the OSD from the Cube, once the aircraft hovers reliably.
  • All-in-one alternative: CubePilot Herelink, a handheld controller that carries RC, HD video and telemetry over one 2.4 GHz link. It's made for the Cube but costs much more than the separate radios.

Setting up the telemetry pair

  1. Plug the air radio into TELEM1 and the ground radio into the laptop's USB port.
  2. Set SERIAL1_PROTOCOL = 2 (MAVLink 2) and SERIAL1_BAUD = 57.
  3. In Mission Planner's SiK Radio screen, load both radios. Give them the same NETID (unique to your team), the same air speed (64 kbps is a good start) and the same frequency range, and turn on ECC and MAVLink framing. Save both.
  4. Both radios' LEDs go solid green when they link up. Close Mission Planner and connect the dashboard at 57600 baud.

Antennas and interference

  • Mount the antennas vertically, away from carbon (the spar and tubes block radio), and not alongside each other. Keep the 2.4 GHz RC antennas away from the 2.4 GHz video or Herelink antennas.
  • Keep the video transmitter as far from the GPS as the fuselage allows. A 5.8 GHz VTX is a well-known source of GPS interference. Check the satellite count with the VTX on and off.
  • Never power a VTX or telemetry radio without its antenna fitted; it can damage the transmitter.

Range test before flying

With the aircraft on the ground and props off, walk the ground radio away with the dashboard open. Note where the fade margin drops below about 20 and where packet loss starts. Do the same with the RC link using the transmitter's range-check mode. Fly well inside both distances.

Live dashboard

New: Tri-Tilt GCS, a standalone ground station built for this aircraft. It works with PX4 and ArduPilot (it detects which), and it's one file: save gcs.html to the laptop and double-click it at the field. No internet or install needed (Chrome or Edge). It shows the flight phase (ground → hover → transition → plane), checks before each transition, a live transition timer, motor outputs and tilt on a top view, the CG estimated from hover motor balance, the radio round-trip time, and spoken callouts. There's also a slot for a camera feed.

The project site has a live ground station at /dashboard.html. It talks MAVLink directly to the ground telemetry radio through the browser's Web Serial API, so the laptop needs no other install.

  • Shows: artificial horizon, altitude, airspeed (marked "estimated" when there's no sensor), groundspeed, wind and current head/tailwind, climb, heading, throttle, battery voltage and current, GPS fix and satellites, radio RSSI and fade margin, packet loss, both tilt-rotor angles, motor outputs, RC inputs, and a live map with track, home and the aircraft's heading. ArduPilot's own messages scroll in a log.
  • Controls: arm and disarm (press and hold), every useful mode, one-tap QLAND / QRTL / RTL, and "fly here": click the map, set an altitude, and the aircraft goes there in GUIDED. Switching from a VTOL mode to a forward-flight mode asks for confirmation, because that starts a transition.
  • Logs: "Save .tlog" downloads everything sent and received, which opens in Mission Planner for replay.
  • Demo: the Demo button runs a simulated aircraft so the team can practise on the dashboard without hardware.

Using it in the field

  • Use Chrome or Edge on a laptop. Safari, Firefox and phones don't support Web Serial.
  • Only one program can open the radio at a time, so close Mission Planner first. Use Mission Planner for setup, parameters and missions, and the dashboard for flying.
  • Open the page before you leave internet coverage and keep the tab open. Map tiles need internet, but telemetry and controls don't. For a fully offline copy, run python3 -m http.server in the project folder and open http://localhost:8000/dashboard.html.
  • Check that the dashboard's output channels and tilt endpoints (under Settings) match your SERVOx_FUNCTION and SERVOx_MIN/MAX values. Otherwise the tilt angles shown will be wrong.

Who controls what

  • The pilot holds the RC transmitter for every flight and can take over at any moment by flicking the mode switch. That always overrides the dashboard.
  • The ground station operator watches the dashboard, calls out battery, link and altitude, and sends commands only when the pilot agrees.
  • Start with FS_GCS_ENABL = 0, which ignores loss of the ground station, and keep RC failsafe as the primary failsafe. Turn on GCS failsafe only once you fly missions that depend on the telemetry link.

Pre-flight checks and test ladder

No flight happens until every box is ticked, and each rung of the ladder is passed before the next.

Must-checks

  • Every motor in the right position and spinning the right way; front pair counter-rotate
  • Props on the right way round and tight
  • Tilt servos correct: yaw tilts the front motors in opposite directions; FBWA swings both fully forward
  • Rear motor stops at full forward tilt
  • Servo endpoints calibrated; no stall buzz at either end
  • Tilt play under 1–2° by hand
  • CG checked with the battery in, on the hover balance point
  • Failsafe tested: switch off the transmitter and check the aircraft goes to QLAND or RTL
  • Telemetry linked: dashboard shows the right mode, attitude and battery; fade margin healthy; packet loss near 0%
  • GPS 3D fix with 10+ satellites, home position shown on the dashboard map
  • Pilot and ground station operator agree who sends which commands
  • Battery charged, voltage logged, spare prints and props on the field
  • Everyone behind the pilot; fire-safe bag for the LiPo

Test ladder

  1. Props-off bench test in every mode, including mode changes sent from the dashboard
  2. Telemetry and RC range test on the ground
  3. Tethered hover at 20–30 cm (tie to heavy weights, lines short enough that it cannot flip)
  4. Untethered hover at 1–2 m in QSTABILIZE, then QHOVER
  5. Hover with partial tilt (Q_TILT_MAX limited)
  6. Full transition at 30 m or more, into the wind on a calm day, with the switch back to QHOVER pre-briefed as the bailout

Log every flight: date, battery, mode, what happened, the ArduPilot log file name and the dashboard .tlog file name.

Risks and mitigations

The two risks most likely to sink the project are a CG that doesn't match the hover balance point and a sloppy tilt mechanism. Both are caught on the bench, not in the air.

Risk Early sign Mitigation
CG off the hover balance point Hover needs heavy pitch trim, or one end saturates Fix b = 2a in CAD; move the battery tray to trim; check before every flight
Tilt slop or MG995 jitter Hover wobble, buzzing servos Bearing-supported pivot, tightest servos, clamped horns, spare servo
Overweight airframe Hover throttle above 65% LW-PLA wing skins, weigh every printed section, carbon spar instead of thick walls
Servo brown-out Flight controller resets when servos move Dedicated BEC of 5 A or more; check voltage under load on the bench
Tilt mount cracks Visible whitening or play after a hard landing PETG or PLA+ with high infill; 2 spares of every mount on the field
Transition crash Altitude loss as the motors tilt SITL practice first, transition into the wind, Q_TRANS_FAIL, Q_ASSIST_ALT, high altitude, bailout to QHOVER
Airspeed estimate wrong (no sensor) Estimated airspeed in the log doesn't match ground speed minus wind; mushy controls downwind Transition and turn low only into the wind, cruise at 15–16 m/s, calm days first, add a cheap sensor later
Telemetry link drops Fade margin falling, packet loss rising, "Link lost" on the dashboard Range test first, antennas clear of carbon, RC stays the primary link, RC failsafe tested
Wrong command from the ground Unplanned mode change or transition Confirm prompts on transitions, the operator only acts on the pilot's call, the pilot overrides with the mode switch
VTX interferes with GPS Satellite count drops when the video transmitter powers up Move the VTX away from the GPS mast; check the satellite count with the VTX on and off
Cube and radios make the aircraft too heavy Hover throttle creeps up as electronics go in Weigh the Cube and carrier early; leave video until hover works
Schedule slip Milestones slipping by more than a day Drop the stretch goal, never the must-checks
Print queue too long for one printer Wing sections still printing when electronics are ready Tilt mounts first, wing next, printer running around the clock; borrow a second printer if one is available
Wing sections warp or misalign Gaps or twist when dry-fitted on the spar Brim and enclosure for LW-PLA, alignment pins, dry-fit everything before gluing
